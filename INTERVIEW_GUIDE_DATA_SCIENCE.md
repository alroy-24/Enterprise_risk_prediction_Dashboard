# Interview Guide — Data Science Role
### Enterprise Risk Intelligence Platform (this repo)

> Same project as the Data Analyst guide, different emphasis: here the interviewer cares about
> **modelling decisions, evaluation rigour, and what you'd do with more time** — not the dashboard.
> All numbers are current and verified (post leakage-fix, June 2026).

---

## 1. The 70-second intro (≈175 words — DS framing)

> "I built a **credit-default prediction system** end to end: feature engineering, model training,
> evaluation, and serving.
>
> The dataset is ~2,100 company-period records of real public-company financials with a binary
> high-risk label — base rate around **43%**. I engineered **20+ financial ratios** — leverage,
> liquidity, coverage, profitability, Altman Z — and train two models on an **18-feature set**:
> a **logistic regression as the interpretable baseline** and **XGBoost as the production model**,
> with one-hot encoded industry and region.
>
> The part I'm most proud of: I **found and fixed data leakage**. The label had been constructed
> partly by thresholding a `default_probability` column that was also a model feature. Removing it
> dropped the metrics from suspiciously high to an honest **0.78 ROC-AUC / 0.75 PR-AUC**, which I
> validate on a stratified held-out set with class-imbalance handling.
>
> Everything ships through a **FastAPI service** with a model-quality dashboard — ROC and PR curves,
> calibration, confusion matrix, feature importances — plus stress-testing and Monte-Carlo VaR
> layers on top of the model's predictions."

The leakage sentence is bait. A good interviewer **will** bite. §3 is your prepared deep-dive.

## 3. The data-leakage story (your best 2 minutes — rehearse it)

> "The dataset's `risk_flag` label was generated by a rule: flag a company if at least two of
> seven conditions hold — high debt-to-equity, current ratio below 1, negative margin,
> **default_probability above 0.15**, Altman Z in the distress zone, and so on, plus ~20% random
> flips for noise.
>
> The original feature set fed `default_probability` — and a derived `default_buffer = 1 − p` —
> straight into the model. That's **target leakage**: a feature that directly encodes part of the
> label-generation rule. The model looked great on paper and would have told you nothing in
> production.
>
> I removed both features, added a **stratified split**, and handled the imbalance with
> `class_weight='balanced'` on the logistic model and `scale_pos_weight` on XGBoost. Honest
> performance: 0.78 ROC-AUC. Lower than the leaky number — and infinitely more useful.
>
> The subtle part: the *other* rule inputs — current ratio, Altman Z, debt-to-equity — stay in the
> feature set. They're legitimate observable fundamentals; a label being *correlated* with real
> features is exactly what you want. Leakage is when the feature is the label's **definition**,
> or wouldn't be available at prediction time."

Follow-up they may ask — **"How would you detect leakage in general?"**
> "Suspiciously perfect metrics; single features with dominant importance; features whose names or
> lineage smell like the target; train a model on each feature alone and check AUC; and the
> discipline question — *would I have this value at prediction time?*"

## 4. ML terms you must be able to explain cold

### Evaluation
- **ROC-AUC** — probability the model ranks a random positive above a random negative.
  Threshold-independent. 0.5 = coin flip.
- **PR-AUC / average precision** — area under precision-recall; more informative than ROC when
  positives are rare or when you care about the flagged set's purity. Baseline = base rate (0.43
  here), *not* 0.5 — that's why I report it alongside ROC-AUC.
- **Brier score** — mean squared error of predicted probabilities; measures **calibration** +
  sharpness. 0.19 for XGBoost vs 0.25 for an uninformed predictor at this base rate.
- **Calibration curve** — bin predictions, plot mean predicted vs observed positive rate; the
  dashboard shows 10 quantile bins. A calibrated model's 0.7 means 70% actually default — essential
  if probabilities feed pricing or capital decisions, not just rankings.
- **Confusion matrix** — at 0.5: XGBoost TP 138 / FN 48 / FP 65 / TN 178. In credit risk, FNs
  (missed defaulters) usually cost more than FPs (manual review), so I'd tune the threshold by
  expected cost, not accuracy.
- **Stratified split** — preserves class proportions in train/test; matters whenever the base
  rate isn't 50% and especially for small test sets.

### Models
- **Logistic regression** — linear model through a sigmoid; coefficients = log-odds per unit;
  L2-regularised (C=1.0), `lbfgs`. The audit-friendly baseline regulators like.
- **XGBoost / gradient boosting** — ensemble of shallow trees, each fit to the gradient of the
  loss on the previous ensemble's errors. Config: max_depth 4, 120 trees, lr 0.08, subsample 0.9,
  colsample 0.8. Wins on tabular data; captures interactions and non-linearity that ratios have
  (e.g., leverage only matters when liquidity is also weak).
- **Class imbalance** — `class_weight='balanced'` reweights the loss inversely to class frequency;
  `scale_pos_weight = neg/pos` does the same for XGBoost. Alternatives: SMOTE, under-sampling,
  threshold-moving. With a 43% base rate the imbalance is mild — reweighting suffices.
- **One-hot encoding** — industry/region become binary columns; `handle_unknown='ignore'` so an
  unseen category at inference doesn't crash the pipeline.
- **sklearn Pipeline** — preprocessing + model fused into one object, so the exact same transform
  runs at train and inference; prevents train/serve skew.
- **Feature importance** — XGBoost gain-based importances, with one-hot columns summed back to
  their parent feature for readability. Caveat to volunteer: importances are biased toward
  high-cardinality features and say nothing about direction — that's why the app also has **SHAP**.
- **SHAP** — Shapley values from game theory; per-prediction additive attribution. The Streamlit
  app uses it for "why is this company risky" explanations.

### Beyond the classifier
- **Monte Carlo VaR** — simulate thousands of return paths from a fitted distribution, read the
  loss quantile. Also implemented: historical (empirical quantile) and parametric (normal
  assumption — fast but underestimates fat tails).
- **Stress testing** — shock raw inputs (revenue −40%, interest +50% under basel_severe), re-run
  the **same inference pipeline** on shocked data, compare probability distributions. The model is
  the simulator.
- **Rating mapping** — fixed probability thresholds → AAA–D buckets. Known limitation: thresholds
  aren't calibrated to this portfolio's 43% base rate, so investment grade is tiny (~3%).

## 5. Hard questions and honest answers

**"Why is 0.78 AUC acceptable? It seems low."**
> "It's honest. The label has ~20% deliberate random flips, which puts a hard ceiling on achievable
> AUC — no model can learn pure noise. On real default data with this feature set I'd expect
> mid-0.8s; published credit models live in the 0.75–0.85 band. I'd rather defend 0.78 than
> explain a leaky 0.99."

**"Why no cross-validation?"**
> "Deliberate trade-off: the model retrains at every API startup, so I kept the training path lean
> with a single stratified hold-out. With more time I'd add 5-fold CV for the *reported* metrics
> and confidence intervals, while keeping the serving path fast. With 2,144 rows, k-fold is cheap
> offline."

**"There are multiple rows per company across periods. Problem?"**
> "Yes — potential entity leakage: the same company can appear in train and test in different
> periods, and its fundamentals are autocorrelated. The proper fix is a **grouped split** on
> company_id (GroupKFold) or a **temporal split** — train on earlier periods, test on later. That's
> top of my improvement list and I'd expect the honest AUC to dip slightly."
> *(Know this cold — it's the strongest probe of the project and saying it before they do wins
> the exchange.)*

**"How would you pick the classification threshold?"**
> "Cost-based. Assign costs to FN (missed defaulter — credit loss) and FP (manual review), then
> choose the threshold minimising expected cost on the validation set; or hit a recall target set
> by risk appetite. 0.5 is just the default, not a decision."

**"Is the model calibrated?"**
> "Reasonably — Brier 0.19 and the reliability curve tracks the diagonal with some wobble in the
> mid-range. If decisions consumed raw probabilities I'd add **isotonic regression or Platt
> scaling** on a held-out calibration fold."

**"Logistic regression has higher recall than XGBoost. So why XGBoost?"**
> "That's a threshold artefact, not model quality — the balanced class weights push LR's
> probabilities up, so at 0.5 it flags more. At matched precision, XGBoost dominates: higher AUC
> and PR-AUC means a better ranking everywhere. If I needed 79% recall from XGBoost I'd just lower
> its threshold and still keep better precision than LR."

**"How does this get to production?"**
> "FastAPI serves it; the trained pipeline lives in process memory and rebuilds on `/refresh`.
> Tests in pytest, CI via GitHub Actions, Dockerfile for packaging, MLflow hooks for experiment
> tracking. Real next steps: persist the model artefact instead of retraining at startup, version
> it in a registry, and monitor input drift — Evidently is already stubbed in the config."

**"What's synthetic about the data?"** *(do not get caught here)*
> "Financial statement lines are from real public filings; operational incidents, compliance
> counts, and the risk label itself are synthetic. The label is rule-generated with noise — which I
> can defend because I can explain exactly how it was generated and what that implies about the
> metric ceiling."

## 6. Weaknesses to volunteer (saying them first = senior signal)

1. **Label quality** — synthetic, rule-based; the model partly re-learns the rule. Real default
   histories would change everything.
2. **Entity/temporal leakage risk** — multiple periods per company with a random split (see §5).
3. **No CV / no confidence intervals** on reported metrics.
4. **Rating thresholds not calibrated** to the portfolio base rate.
5. **VaR uses synthetic returns** derived from predicted probabilities — methodologically the three
   estimators are real, but the input series is illustrative.
6. **No hyperparameter search** — sensible defaults; would add Optuna with grouped CV.

## 7. If they ask about the AI-assisted workflow

Be direct: AI tools accelerated the build; you own the design decisions and can defend every
number. Useful line:

> "AI got me boilerplate fast, but it also confidently shipped the data leakage — catching that
> required understanding the label-generation rule and questioning metrics that looked too good.
> That's exactly the judgement layer the human owes the project."

This turns the "vibecoded" risk into your strongest answer: it shows you know where AI-generated
analysis breaks and that you verify rather than trust.
